Why hot work permits matter

Published Aug 2026 3 min read Includes downloadable permit
Applies to welding, cutting, grinding, brazing, and similar work
See also: DR–2026–015

The scale of the problem

Hot work is one of the most consistently cited causes of large property fire losses in industrial and commercial settings. The pattern is familiar: a routine welding or cutting job, a spark or hot fragment lands somewhere unnoticed, and a fire develops well after the crew has packed up and left — often outside the window anyone was watching.

What a permit system actually does

A hot work permit isn't paperwork for its own sake. It forces three things to happen before work starts that otherwise get skipped under time pressure: a genuine check of whether hot work is even necessary, a deliberate look at what's combustible in and around the work area, and a named person accountable for watching the area after the torch goes off.

Most hot-work-related losses trace back to one of these steps being skipped, not to the permit form itself being poorly designed. The value is in the discipline the process imposes, not the document.

Where sites tend to fall short

  • Fire watch ending the moment the work finishes, rather than continuing long enough to catch a delayed ignition
  • Combustible construction — timber framing, insulated panels with combustible cores — not being assessed, only loose materials in the immediate area
  • Permits authorised as a formality without anyone seriously considering non-hot-work alternatives
